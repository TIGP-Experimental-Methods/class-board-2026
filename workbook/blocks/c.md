# Section C — The front panel board (your section)

**What it does.** The front panel is the face of the instrument and the board everything leaves through. It is a second printed circuit board, 180 × 100 mm and **four layers**, that sits flat on the back of the main board on 2.54 mm pin-header links and four M3 stand-offs. Every signal a user touches arrives here: the SMA field, the TTL screw-terminal strip, the transmit-coil terminal, the Qwiic connector, the OLED and the three status LEDs. Two circuits of your own live here as well — the **two isolated inputs**, which let the instrument take a trigger from equipment that does not share its ground, and the **module header**, seven buffered 5 V outputs that let the instrument switch things outside the box. If your section is wrong, every other section's signal is wrong on its way in or out.

**Parts in your zone.**
- **The SMA field (17 connectors in a 3 × 6 grid, 18 mm pitch).** Vertical 50 Ω SMA sockets on the panel's outer face: row 1 AO1 AO2 TRIG AUX TX FAST1 · row 2 AI1 AI2 AI3 AI4 RX FAST2 · row 3 AI5 AI6 AI7 AI8 SPARE (one grid position is a blank spare). Each one is a net that comes across a link header from Section A or Section B, so the panel is where their signals are either kept clean or spoilt.
- **The link headers (the instructor's, but you route to them).** 2×20 female headers on the panel's inner face, opposite the male headers on the back of the main board. The rule on all of them: **odd pin = signal, even pin = its return** — AGND for analog, GND for digital. Never move a net to a pin whose neighbour is the wrong return.
- **The TTL strip and the coil terminal.** A 10-way KF128 screw terminal for TTL1–8 plus two grounds, and a 2-way KF128 for the transmit coil beside the TX socket. Both sit **flush with a panel edge with the wire entry facing off the edge** — nothing points inwards.
- **The OLED, the LEDs and the Qwiic.** A 0.96 in 128 × 64 I²C OLED on a vertical socket, held by four M2 stand-offs through holes in its footprint and clear of the solder tails of any link header; three LEDs (power, WiFi, activity); a vertical Qwiic (JST-SH 4-pin) for I²C accessories.
- **The two isolated inputs (yours).** Two **6N137** optocouplers — a logic-gate output part, 5 kV isolation and a 75 ns propagation delay, which is why the instrument can timestamp an external edge instead of merely noticing it. Each input is a 2-pin screw terminal that accepts **5–24 V**; the input LED is fed through a series 1N4148W (reverse blocking), a 220 Ω resistor and a **two-transistor MMBT5551 current source** (with its 10 kΩ bias and the 100 Ω ∥ 1 kΩ emitter set) so that 5 V and 24 V give the same LED current, about 6.8 mA. On the output side: +3V3, a 1 kΩ pull-up and a 100 nF bypass; the outputs cross to the microcontroller's GPIO 16 and 17 (a GPIO is a general-purpose pin on the microcontroller), active low.
- **The module header (yours).** A **2×6 shrouded box header** on the panel — the shroud and its key mean the plug goes in one way only. It carries **+5 V on two pins, ground on two pins, seven 5 V TTL outputs, MODULE OUT 1–7, and one reserved spare pin** — the expander has no port left for an eighth line, so that pin is tied low and always reads low. The seven live lines come from the I²C expander on Section B's sheet at 3.3 V, through a **74AHCT541 octal buffer running from +5 V** (an AHCT part is the one that accepts 3.3 V logic levels and puts out 5 V ones), each output through a **47 Ω** series resistor. The header drives the opto-isolated inputs of an external module, which typically want about 5 mA each.

**What the module header is for.** The instrument can switch things that are not on the board: a relay module, a solid-state relay, a valve driver, a stepper driver — anything with an opto-isolated logic input. The one to start from is the **Jinhua 4-channel relay module** ([#41604](https://jin-hua.com.tw/page/product/show.aspx?num=41604&kw=%e7%b9%bc%e9%9b%bb&lang=TW), "4路12V繼電器模組", NT$150 ≈ US$4.7): four single-pole double-throw relays rated 10 A at 250 V AC or 30 V DC, opto-isolated inputs with a jumper that selects high- or low-level triggering, about 5 mA of trigger current, 73 × 50 × 20 mm. Wire its input pins to MODULE OUT lines and its input ground to a ground pin of the header. That module's **relay coils run from 12 V and need their own 12 V supply**; Jinhua also sell a 5 V version of the same module, whose coils can run from the header's +5 V — check the current it draws before you rely on that. What the relays themselves switch is beyond our board: **if you intend to switch wall power, that wiring is your own responsibility and must be done by someone qualified to do it.**

**Reference numbers.** The isolated inputs keep their 4xx numbers (U401/U402, J411/J412 and their passives); the panel connectors, the buffer and the module header carry the names printed on the board. Keep them as you find them — the footprints on the board carry the same names.

**Your zone on the PCB** (the region of the physical board that is yours to route)**.** The whole front-panel board is your zone, and it is **four layers**: the outer face carries the connectors and the silkscreen the user reads, the two inner layers are ground and stay unbroken, the inner face carries the link headers. Four things decide whether it works:
- **Returns.** Every signal crossing a link header has its own return pin beside it, and the return has to be continuous copper the whole way. Analog nets (the SMA field, RX, TX) return on **AGND**; digital nets (TTL, the module outputs, the OLED, the Qwiic) return on **GND**. Do not let a digital return current find its way home under the analog field.
- **The isolation band.** The input side of each optocoupler — terminal, resistor, diode, transistor pair, LED pins — is the `ISO_IN` net class: **2.5 mm clearance to everything and no copper of any other net under it on any layer**. That gap is the whole point of the part; one careless ground pour defeats a barrier the manufacturer guarantees to 5 kV. The design rule check tests it, and so does your reviewer. *Rule of thumb: isolation is geometry, not a component.*
- **Mechanics.** Every screw terminal flush with an edge and taking its wire off the edge; the OLED fixed by its four M2 holes and not over the solder tails of a link header; the SMA bodies clear of each other at 18 mm pitch and clear of the M3 stand-offs at the corners; the shrouded header turned so its cable can leave the instrument.
- **Silkscreen.** This is the face of the instrument. Every connector labelled with the name a user will look for — AI1, MODULE OUT 3, the +5 V pin of the header — and still legible with the plugs in.

## Your design number (E3)
**The module-output drive budget.** The 74AHCT541 runs from +5 V and each output feeds one opto-isolated module input that wants **5 mA**, through your 47 Ω series resistor. Compute (a) the voltage dropped across the 47 Ω at 5 mA, (b) the current the buffer must supply with **all seven** outputs on at once, (c) how that compares with the part's guaranteed per-output drive current and with the maximum current its supply and ground pins are rated for, (d) the resistance the module's own input circuit must have for 5 mA to flow from 5 V through an LED that drops about 1.2 V, and (e) one sentence on what the 47 Ω is actually protecting. *Expected magnitude:* about 0.24 V; 35 mA in total; the part is specified at ±8 mA per output and 50 mA through the supply pin, so seven channels on is inside the rating but not by much; roughly 700–800 Ω inside the module; and the 47 Ω limits the surge into a cable's capacitance and the current if an output meets a short. Working, a unit and a one-line conclusion into `docs/students/<name>/notes.md`.

## Your number (E13b)
**Command-to-edge latency.** Wire **MODULE OUT 1** to **isolated input 1** and its ground return; command the output from the phone; the firmware timestamps the optocoupler edge. The hardware takes tens of nanoseconds through the 6N137, so what you are measuring is the software and I²C path — expect a few milliseconds, and say what dominates it. The second number, if you have time: the +5 V rail at the module header with no load and with all seven outputs on, measured at the pins.

## Your software (E11)
Commands `module {n, on}` (n = 1…7), `module_all {on}`, `opto_reset`; status `module1…module7`, `opto1`, `opto2` (edge counts) and `opto1_level`, `opto2_level`. Then the part only your section can do: the **expander driver** (the module outputs hang off the I²C expander, so an output command is a register write, not a pin write — read, modify, write, and keep a shadow copy of the register), an **edge counter** on the isolated inputs, and **safe defaults**: every output off at reset and after a reconnection, and one call that puts the instrument back into a known state. SIM: fake optocoupler edges every few seconds. Alarm rule: an `opto1` count jump → `notify`. Pair with Section B in Workshop 3: their TTL line into your optocoupler.

## What this section teaches
- **Isolation is geometry.** Creepage is a distance on the board, and one careless pour defeats a barrier the part is guaranteed to hold to 5 kV.
- **A buffer is a boundary.** It converts logic levels, it supplies current the source cannot, and it is the part that dies instead of the expander when the outside world does something stupid.
- **The panel is where returns are won or lost.** A signal is a loop; the pin beside it is half the circuit — and a microvolt receiver and eight switching outputs share this board.
- **Connectors decide whether an instrument is usable.** Keyed, labelled, flush with the edge, wires off the edge, and a plug that only goes in one way.
- **What you cannot do on the board, you can do beyond it.** Seven buffered lines and a ground become relays, drivers and valves — the instrument ends at the header, and what hangs off it is a design of its own.
